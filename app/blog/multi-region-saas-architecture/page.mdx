"Just deploy it to another region" is where most multi-region conversations
start and end. But going global is less about copying infrastructure and more
about deciding **where your data lives, how it stays consistent, and what you're
willing to trade for latency.**

## Start from the requirements, not the map

Three forces usually drive the decision:

- **Data residency** — regulations may require EU user data to stay in the EU.
- **Disaster recovery** — a region can fail; can you keep serving?
- **Latency** — users near a region should get local response times.

These pull in different directions. Residency partitions your data; DR wants it
replicated; latency wants it close to everyone. The architecture is how you
reconcile them.

## Two broad patterns

### Region-partitioned (data stays home)

Each region owns its users' data end to end. A routing layer sends a request to
the region that owns that tenant. This nails residency and keeps things simple —
at the cost of cross-region features being harder.

### Replicated with a primary

One region is the write primary; others are read replicas. Great for read-heavy,
latency-sensitive workloads, but you inherit **replication lag** and must design
for eventual consistency on the read path.

## The trade-offs you can't avoid

- **Consistency vs. latency.** Synchronous cross-region writes are slow;
  asynchronous ones are eventually consistent. Pick per use case, not globally.
- **Routing.** Geo-DNS and edge routing decide who goes where; getting tenant →
  region mapping right is half the battle.
- **Operational cost.** Every region is more to deploy, monitor, and pay for.
  Start with the fewest regions that satisfy the requirements.

## HLD then LLD

I sketch the **high-level design** first — regions, routing, data ownership,
failover story — and only then drop into the **low-level design**: schemas,
replication config, health checks, and the runbook for a region failover. The
HLD keeps the LLD honest.

> Multi-region isn't a switch you flip. It's a set of deliberate trade-offs you
> make with your eyes open — and document so the on-call engineer at 3 a.m.
> understands them too.
